01 / The project

The Norwich Fire District supplies drinking water to roughly 310 homes and 20 businesses from a single drilled well. A large parcel containing a defunct gravel pit, next to the well's source protection area and not owned by the District, is going up for sale, and a likely buyer is an excavating company that would store construction materials and equipment there. Fuel, fluids, and other chemicals stored on permeable gravel close to the well create a real contamination risk, alongside an existing one from deicing salt and vehicle spills on the stretch of I-91 that crosses the protection area.

We are evaluating ways to protect the well, weighing technical performance against cost and benefit, and presenting design alternatives to the Fire District.

02 / Key terms

Risk

Term 001

Contamination risk (municipal water source)

The chance that a harmful substance reaches a public water supply, combined with how serious the consequences would be. A useful way to frame it: source → pathway → receptor.

Here, the source could be stored fuel or road salt, the pathway is infiltration through soil and groundwater flow, and the receptor is the well and everyone who drinks from it. Break any link and the risk drops.

Term 002

Prevention

Actions that reduce the likelihood of contamination happening at all, by removing the hazard or blocking the pathway before anything is released.

Examples: keeping a land use off the property, banning fuel storage in the protection area, or placing a barrier between a spill and the ground.

Term 003

Mitigation

Actions that reduce the consequences once a hazard exists or an incident occurs: limiting how far contamination spreads, how much reaches the well, or how long the supply is affected.

Examples: early-warning monitoring, spill response plans, containment and cleanup, or a backup water source.

Water & land

Term 004

Well

A hole drilled, dug, or bored into the ground to reach groundwater. Water enters through a screened or open section and is pumped to the surface; a sealed casing keeps surface water from running straight down the borehole.

Norwich's supply is a drilled well. Pumping pulls water toward the well from the surrounding aquifer, including from beyond the property line.

Term 005

Watershed

The area of land where rain and snowmelt drain toward a shared outlet such as a stream, lake, or river. Activity anywhere in a watershed (fertilizer, road runoff, spills) can affect water downstream.

For a well, the more relevant boundary is often the recharge area underground, which doesn't always line up with the surface watershed.

Term 006

Source protection area (SPA)

The mapped area around a public water source where land use can affect water quality, generally the land that contributes recharge to the well. Federally this is often called a wellhead protection area.

In Vermont, SPAs are delineated through the state's source protection program (VT DEC) and are typically divided into zones by how close they are to the well. The gravel pit parcel sits right next to Norwich's SPA.

Term 007

Impervious surface (concrete pad)

A surface water can't soak through, such as concrete, asphalt, or rooftops. A concrete pad under stored equipment or materials keeps spills from infiltrating directly into the ground.

The trade-off: water that can't infiltrate becomes runoff, so a pad only works with curbs, drainage, and a place to send what collects (e.g., an oil/water separator). Cracks and joints need maintenance to stay sealed.

Below the surface

Term 008

Groundwater & aquifer

Groundwater is water stored in the pore spaces and fractures of soil and rock below the water table.

An aquifer is a geologic formation that can store and transmit enough groundwater to supply a well. Sand and gravel aquifers yield water easily, but that same openness lets contaminants travel easily too.

Term 009

Esker

A long, winding ridge of sorted sand and gravel left behind by meltwater streams that flowed in tunnels under retreating glaciers. They're common across New England.

Eskers and similar glacial deposits are both excellent aquifers and valuable gravel sources, which is why wells and gravel pits so often end up as neighbors.

Term 010

Darcy's law

Describes how fast groundwater moves through porous material:

Q = K · A · (Δh / L)
v = K · i / ne

Q = flow rate, K = hydraulic conductivity, A = cross-sectional area, Δh/L (or i) = hydraulic gradient, v = average groundwater velocity, ne = effective porosity.

Why it matters here: with v and the distance from the gravel pit to the well, we can estimate travel time (t = L / v), meaning how long a spill would take to reach the well and how much warning monitoring could give. Gravel has one of the highest K values of any natural material, so travel times can be short.

03 / A quick distinction

Remember: Prevention lowers the chance something goes wrong. Mitigation lowers the damage when it does. Monitoring wells don't stop a spill; they buy time to respond. A strong plan usually needs both.

04 / Design alternatives

  • 01. Do nothing. The baseline: the parcel sells and is used as proposed, with no added protection. Every other option is measured against this.
  • 02. Impervious slab / physical barrier. The excavating company buys the parcel, and storage areas sit on a contained concrete pad that keeps spills out of the ground. (Prevention)
  • 03. Monitoring wells. Wells installed on the gravel pit, between the potential source and the supply well, to detect contamination before it arrives. (Mitigation)
  • 04. Purchase and conserve the land. The parcel is bought and protected, likely in partnership with a land trust such as the Upper Valley Land Trust, removing the hazard entirely. (Prevention)

05 / Contaminants of concern

  • 01. Petroleum products such as diesel, gasoline, hydraulic fluid, and motor oil from equipment storage and fueling.
  • 02. Chloride from road salt on I-91. It's highly mobile in groundwater and isn't removed by conventional treatment.
  • 03. Solvents and degreasers used in equipment maintenance.
  • 04. Accidental spills from vehicle crashes on the highway, where the material and volume are unpredictable.
Why gravel matters: Coarse, permeable soils give contaminants a fast, direct path to the water table with little natural filtering along the way. That's what makes a former gravel pit a sensitive place to store chemicals.
